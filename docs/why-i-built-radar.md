# Why I Built Event Opportunity Radar

## The short answer

I started building Event Opportunity Radar because I realized that finding opportunities was not the hardest part. Finding the right opportunities and knowing what to do with them was.

I wanted a system that could help me discover technical events, hackathons, volunteering roles, open-source contribution paths, learning programs, networking opportunities, fellowships, and other ways to gain real experience.

Those opportunities are scattered across official websites, GitHub, event platforms, community pages, organizer websites, and social posts. Finding one link is easy. Repeating the entire search, trust-checking, date-checking, eligibility-checking, skill-checking, and application process every time is not.

So I built Radar for myself first.

## The problem

A normal event listing can tell you:

> An event exists.

That is only the beginning. A person still needs to ask:

- Is it legitimate and still active?
- What are the real start and end dates?
- Is there an application or volunteer route?
- Who should I contact?
- What could I realistically contribute?
- What will I learn?
- Is it worth the time and travel?
- What should I do next?

Radar is designed to move from:

```text
I found something.
        ->
This is trustworthy and relevant.
        ->
Here is a realistic path to participate.
        ->
Here is what I should do next.
```

## Why the name Radar

There are thousands of opportunity signals across the internet. Radar is not meant to become a giant database of everything. It is meant to continuously look across useful sources and surface the signals that deserve attention.

That is why this is an opportunity intelligence system rather than another event calendar.

## What the system does

The interesting part is what happens before an opportunity reaches the interface:

```text
Sources
  -> Source selection and health
  -> Discovery planning
  -> Adapter execution
  -> Validation and normalization
  -> Evidence and verification
  -> Canonical opportunity identity
  -> Action intelligence
  -> Roles, routes, contacts, and prompts
  -> Lifecycle follow-through
```

If ten sources mention the same hackathon, the long-term goal is one canonical opportunity with multiple discovery signals and evidence sources. A social post can be a useful lead without being treated as full verification. A predicted role can be useful guidance without being presented as an organizer-confirmed fact.

## The philosophy

Radar is not designed to optimize for collecting certificates or counting attendance. It is designed around:

```text
Learn -> Contribute -> Network -> Build evidence -> Follow up
```

The goal is to help someone move from passive opportunity seeking to active contribution. A useful outcome might be a new skill, a real contribution, a relationship, a documented project, a GitHub contribution, or a stronger portfolio story.

## Why it can help other people

I built it for myself first, but the problem is not unique to me. Students and early-career people often know they need experience, learning, and relationships, but do not know where to look or which opportunities are worth pursuing.

The public repository shares the open engine and product direction while keeping private applications, notes, contacts, certificates, and personal tracking separate.

## The 90-second explanation

I started building Event Opportunity Radar because I kept running into the same problem: I wanted to find meaningful opportunities outside of simply applying for jobs, such as technical events, hackathons, volunteering, open-source contributions, learning programs, networking, and community work.

The opportunities were everywhere, but scattered. I had to search official websites, GitHub, event platforms, community pages, organizer sites, and social media. Even after finding something, I still had to determine whether it was legitimate, current, relevant to my skills, realistic for me to contribute to, and worth my time.

So I started building a personal system that could discover opportunities from multiple sources, preserve evidence, reduce duplicates, understand the opportunity, and help decide the next action. That is where the name Radar comes from: many signals exist, but the system should help surface the ones worth attention.

The project is deliberately more than an event listing. It separates discovery from verification, facts from predictions, and a found link from a realistic contribution path. It can show event windows, application routes, contacts, predicted roles, preparation strategies, prompts for external GPT tools, and a lifecycle for following through after discovery.

The long-term goal is to help people move from finding opportunities to using them well: learn, contribute, network, build evidence, and follow up.
